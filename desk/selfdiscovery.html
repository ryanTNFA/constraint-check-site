Self-discovery

Are firms held back from trying new products?

The idea, from the research

How a missing first try can hold growth back

Growing means making new things. Rich countries don’t just make more of the same; they make different products. For a poorer country the task is rarely to invent something. It is to find out which of the products the world already makes its own firms can make at low enough cost to sell. Hausmann and Rodrik call this self-discovery: finding out an economy’s own cost structure.

The first firm pays; the others copy. Finding out is costly. If the first firm to try fails, it bears the whole loss. If it succeeds, others see that it works and follow, and the first firm keeps little of the gain. So firms try too few new things, even when trying would pay off for the country as a whole.

New products need inputs nobody supplies yet. A new export may need its own standards, certification, cold storage, a port procedure or trained workers. These differ from one product to the next and are hard to know in advance, so a government can’t supply them all. It has to choose.

A related idea: distance. Later work on the “product space” adds a second reason. Products that use similar know-how sit close together. A country that already makes many products near a new one has a short jump; a country far from everything has a long one, and the missing know-how and suppliers may need several firms to move at once. That is a coordination problem, which this tool treats as a separate area (coordination failures).

Sources: Hausmann and Rodrik, “Economic Development as Self-Discovery” (2003) and “Doomed to Choose: Industrial Policy as Predicament” (2006); Hidalgo, Klinger, Barabási and Hausmann, “The Product Space Conditions the Development of Nations” (2007).

The measures

    Since rubric 0.2.8 the discovery gap can carry the rating on its own: it is the one gate-tested measure here, and the others inform the lean. Even so, a country with few new products may be held back by something else, such as power or roads, which is why the next section gives the rating its context. The firm surveys count products new to the firm, not new to the country, so they are only a rough guide.

    The other direction

    From the camels to the water

    The camels-and-hippos test usually starts from a suspected constraint and asks whether the products that need it do badly. This runs it the other way round, as the Growth Lab’s analysts do by eye: which products does this country export far more than its income predicts, which products should it export by its income but does not at all, and what do the two groups need? The missing ones are the hippos a desert never shows. This is a hint about what to ask, not a verdict and not a list of sectors to back.

    What would settle it

    Evidence national statistics can’t give: suggestions

    • Talk to the first movers. Firms that tried a new product or market in the last five years: what stopped them, and did others copy them before they recovered their costs?
    • Map the missing inputs. For two or three nearby products, which specific public inputs are missing: standards, certification, logistics, regulation, skills?
    • Go further with the research tools. The Harvard Growth Lab’s Atlas of Economic Complexity covers the product space in far more depth than this page.
    The New Foreign Assistance’s view · not part of any rating

    Why this matters for U.S. assistance

    If self-discovery is what holds growth back, the most useful help is narrow and temporary: share the risk of the first serious try at a new product, tie that support to results and withdraw it when the try fails, and fix the specific public inputs the product needs so that others can follow. Hausmann and Rodrik stress that discipline: support without it invites waste. This is very different from subsidizing whole sectors indefinitely. It is also why diagnosis comes first: backing first movers makes sense only once the more common problems have been ruled out.
