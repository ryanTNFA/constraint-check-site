How Constraint Check works, and how we test it

About this prototype

What it is, and what it is not

Constraint Check applies growth diagnostics, the method of Hausmann, Rodrik and Velasco behind the Millennium Challenge Corporation's constraints analyses, to public cross-country data for 217 economies. For each country it says which standard explanations for weak private investment the public evidence argues against, which deserve a closer look, and which it cannot settle, with what would settle them.

It is a triage tool built by The New Foreign Assistance (TNFA). It does not estimate growth effects, rank countries or projects, or recommend funding. A rating of “worth a closer look” means look here, not “this binds”; only field work, microdata and judgment can establish that.

It is a prototype. Its ratings are uncalibrated. Most areas in most countries are “can’t tell”, because public cross-country data cannot settle them; every such rating says why.

The New Foreign Assistance’s view

Why this way of choosing: the MCC model, and its limits

The Millennium Challenge Corporation (MCC) is a U.S. agency that gives large grants to well-governed poor countries to remove what holds their economies back, such as roads, power and land rules. Several of its habits are worth copying across U.S. assistance:

  • It diagnoses before it spends. Each partnership starts with an analysis of what most constrains growth, done with the partner government, and projects are chosen against it.
  • It picks partners that reform. A public scorecard of policy indicators decides who qualifies. It is meant to give governments a reason to improve; how much it does is debated.
  • It asks whether a project pays. Investments must show an economic return that justifies their cost.
  • It is finite by design. Each partnership has a fixed five-year clock and an end date.
  • It publishes its results, good and bad, including independent evaluations.

The model also has limits, and they matter for anyone who adopts it:

  • It is slow to put together: too slow when a need is urgent.
  • Its scorecard leaves out many fragile or contested countries where the United States still has good reasons to engage.
  • Diagnoses can drift toward what the agency can fund, often infrastructure, and the link from diagnosis to the projects finally chosen has sometimes been loose.
  • Returns measured after the fact have often come in below those projected, and the five-year clock pushes toward projects that fit it.
  • Carrying projects out through partner governments can stall on procurement and capacity, and what happens after a compact ends is uncertain.

Constraint Check takes the first step of the model, the diagnosis, and makes a quick, public first read of it available for any country. MCC’s constraints analyses rest on months of fieldwork with the partner government; an automated read from public data is a large simplification, and the site’s ratings are not yet tested against those analyses. A full diagnosis still needs people on the ground; the aim is that they start with sharper questions.

The New Foreign Assistance holds that sustained growth is the only proven cure for extreme poverty, that aid should strengthen the public goods that let private enterprise flourish, and that programs should end when their job is done. The ratings on this site follow the method and the data, not these views. thenewforeignassistance.org

Validation

How we test it

  1. A retrospective against published diagnostics: MCC's 43 constraints analyses and the Harvard Growth Lab's country growth diagnostics. The tool is run on data truncated to what each analysis could have seen. The design, the metrics and the pass and fail thresholds were written down and dated on 30 October 2026, before any comparison. The main measure is how often the tool says “probably not” about a constraint the analysts found binding. The results will be published whatever they are.
  2. A prospective test against diagnostics now in progress, with the tool's ratings recorded before the diagnostics are published.
  3. A panel of economists given the same public information.

None has reported yet, so the tool has no track record. Treat every rating as a starting point.

Evidence settings

Three readings: Cautious, Standard and Exploratory

Every country page can show three readings of the same evidence. They show how much a rating depends on judgment calls about how much evidence is enough.

  • Standard is the default, and the only reading to cite.
  • Cautious needs more before it rates anything: three independent measures instead of two, and stronger scores either way.
  • Exploratory makes it easier to flag an area for a closer look. It never makes it easier to say “probably not” or “ruled out”, because wrongly ruling out a real constraint costs more than taking an extra look.

Because of that, a rating can move between “can’t tell” and a verdict as the reading changes, but never from “worth a closer look” to “probably not”. Every rating carries one mark: holds under every reading, depends on the reading, or depends on the comparison countries. Click or tap the mark to see the rating under each reading.

None of the three readings has been tested against expert diagnoses yet. When the retrospective reports, each will show how often it wrongly rules out a constraint the experts found binding, and how often it flags one they found.
